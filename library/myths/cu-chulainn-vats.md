# Cú Chulainn and the Three Vats

When Cú Chulainn was seven years old he heard a druid say that whoever took up arms that day would be famous for ever, though his life would be short. He went straight to King Conchobar and demanded weapons. He broke fifteen sets of spears and shields just by shaking them, until the king gave him his own. Then he took a chariot and drove to the border of Ulster.

There he did what heroes do in the old stories. He fought the three sons of Nechta Scéne, who had killed more Ulstermen than were left alive, and he killed them and took their heads. On the way home he ran down two stags and tied one to the back of the chariot. He brought down a flock of wild swans with stones from his sling and tied them on too, so that they flew above him, still alive, on their cords.

And all the while the battle-rage was on him. The Irish called it the *ríastrad*, the warp-spasm. It turned his body inside out: one eye sank into his head and the other bulged out onto his cheek, his hair stood on end with a spark at the tip of each strand, and a column of dark blood rose from the crown of his head like smoke from a ship's mast. He came racing toward Emain Macha like that, howling, and he swore he would kill anyone who came against him.

The watchman on the wall cried out, and the king understood at once that the boy could not stop. Whatever he had been at the border, he was still at war, and the war would be with them.

So Queen Mugain led the women of the court out to meet him, all of them bare-breasted, holding nothing. The boy was seven. He hid his face in the chariot. And while he could not look, the warriors seized him and plunged him into a vat of cold water.

The vat burst its staves and hoops. They put him in a second vat, and the water boiled up in bubbles as big as fists. They put him in a third, and it grew warm, and then only pleasantly warm, and he came out of it a boy again.

They dressed him in a blue cloak with a silver brooch and sat him at the king's knee, and from then on that was his place.

The story is from the boyhood deeds of the hero, told inside the great Irish epic of the cattle raid. The fight takes a morning. Coming home from it takes three vats, a queen, and a whole court willing to meet the heat without a weapon.
