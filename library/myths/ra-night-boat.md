# Ra's Night Journey

Every morning the sun god Ra rises in the east in his day boat, and all day he sails across the body of the sky goddess Nut, whose arched form is the heavens. At noon he is at his strongest, a falcon-headed king with the disc of the sun on his head. By evening he is old and slow. He comes to the western horizon, where the mountains open, and there he changes boats.

The night boat carries him into the Duat, the underworld, and the Egyptians who painted the walls of their tombs knew its geography hour by hour. There are twelve gates, one for each hour of the night, and each is guarded by a serpent or a goddess who must be named. Beyond them are rivers of fire, sandbanks where the boat runs aground and has to be hauled by jackal-headed gods, caves where the dead lie sleeping. As Ra passes through, the dead wake. They stand up in their coffins, stretch, and breathe again for the one hour his light is with them. When he moves on, they lie down and sleep, and they grieve at his going.

Ra does not sail alone. Thoth is aboard, and Isis with her magic, and old gods whose names are powerful. And at the prow stands Set, the troublesome god of storms, whose one great job each night is to fight.

Because somewhere in the dark, usually around the seventh hour, the serpent Apep rises out of the waters. Apep is chaos, the thing that was there before the world was made and wants to swallow it again. His coils are vast. He drinks the river so the boat runs aground, and he stares at the sun's companions with a gaze that freezes them. Every night Set drives a spear into him, and Isis binds him with spells, and the goddesses cut his coils, and the boat moves on. Every night Apep is defeated, and every night he comes back.

In the deepest hour of the night, Ra's tired old body meets the body of Osiris, lord of the dead, and for a moment they are one: the living sun and the dead king. From that meeting the sun is renewed. Then the boat sails on toward the east, and the last gate opens, and he is reborn as the scarab Khepri, rolling the new sun up over the horizon the way a beetle rolls its ball of earth.

Priests in the temples recited spells against Apep through the night to help. The sunrise was not taken for granted. It was won.
