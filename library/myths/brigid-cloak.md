# Brigid's Cloak

Brigid was a girl who gave everything away. The stories say that even as a child she couldn't pass a beggar without giving him something, and when she had nothing of her own she gave away her father's things: his butter, his meat, even, once, his sword, which she gave to a leper. Her father was a chieftain in Leinster, and he was not pleased.

When she was grown, Brigid wanted to build a house for women who wanted to pray and work and care for the poor. She found the place she wanted, on the plains of Kildare, near a great oak tree. The land belonged to the King of Leinster. So Brigid went to the king and asked him for it.

The king was a hard man, and not generous. He looked at this young woman and her few companions and laughed. How much land did she want?

"Only as much as my cloak will cover," said Brigid.

The king thought that was very little, and it would cost him nothing to agree. "Very well," he said. "Take as much as your cloak will cover."

Brigid took off her cloak and handed it to four of her women. Each took a corner. They faced north, south, east and west, and began to walk. And the cloak began to spread. It spread across the grass as they walked. It spread over the hill and the field and the woods beyond. It went on growing, green and wide, until it covered a great stretch of the Curragh of Kildare, many acres. The king's men shouted. The king himself fell to his knees and begged Brigid to call her women back before she had all of Leinster.

She did. And the king, who had seen enough to be humbled, gave her the land freely. He became generous from that day, the story says, and a friend to the poor.

At Kildare Brigid built her church and her house, and under the oak her women kept a fire burning that was never allowed to go out, tended by nineteen women in turn, and on the twentieth night by Brigid herself. It burned for hundreds of years.

Brigid of Kildare was a real abbess, living around the year 500. She also carries the name and the fire of an older goddess, Brigid of poetry, smithcraft and healing, whose feast was the first of February, Imbolc, when the ewes come into milk and the light starts to come back. On that day, in Ireland, people still weave Brigid's crosses from rushes and hang them over the door.
