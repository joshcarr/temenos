# Selene and Endymion

Selene is the Moon herself. Every night she yokes her white horses and drives her chariot up out of the eastern ocean and across the sky, and the light that falls from her veil is the light you see on the fields.

One night, driving low over the hills of Caria, she looked down into a cave on Mount Latmus and saw a young shepherd asleep among his sheep. His name was Endymion. He lay on his back with one arm flung over his head, the way people sleep when they are very tired and very young. She stopped her horses. She came down out of the sky and stood at the mouth of the cave and looked at him for a long time.

After that she came every night. The Greeks say she would leave her chariot on the hillside, slip into the cave, and lie down beside him, and go back up into the sky before dawn. Some tellings say she bore him fifty daughters, which may be the fifty months between the great games at Olympia. Some say nothing happened at all except the looking.

Then the trouble came that always comes in these stories. Endymion was mortal. He would get old. He would die. Selene could not bear it.

What happened next depends on who is telling it. Some say Selene asked Zeus to keep Endymion as he was, and Zeus granted it by putting him into an endless sleep, so that he would never age and never die. Others say Zeus offered Endymion anything he wanted, and Endymion chose it himself: to sleep for ever, without growing old, beautiful and untroubled. Either way, the shepherd sleeps.

He is sleeping still, they say, in the cave on Mount Latmus, and she still comes to him. She doesn't wake him. That was never what she wanted. She wanted to look at him, and to have him there to be looked at, and to go back to her work in the sky knowing he would be in the same place tomorrow night.

Later poets loved this story and made it into a kind of riddle. Is it the perfect love, faithful and endless? Or is it a love that only works so long as one of them never wakes up? The ancient writers left it open. The Moon keeps her visits, the shepherd keeps his sleep, and neither has to change.

On clear nights over that coast, when the Moon hangs low over the hills, people used to say she had stopped her horses again.
