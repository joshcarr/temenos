# Asclepius and the Serpent Staff

Asclepius was born on a funeral pyre. His mother, Coronis, had been loved by Apollo and had loved someone else besides, and the god's anger, or his sister Artemis's arrows, killed her before the child came. As the flames rose around her, Apollo relented enough to reach into the fire and pull out his son, alive. Then he did what the gods often did with children they couldn't raise: he carried the baby up Mount Pelion to the cave of Chiron the centaur.

Chiron had taught heroes to fight and to sing. To this boy he taught the other things he knew. Which roots to dig at which hour. How to set a bone so it knits straight. How to read a fever by the skin and the breath. How to listen to a sick person until they told you what was really wrong. Chiron knew so much about wounds partly because he carried one himself, and Asclepius learned at the side of a teacher who could cure everyone but himself.

The pupil outgrew the teacher. Asclepius went down into the towns and healed what no one else could heal. People said a snake once came to him while he was treating a patient, and he killed it, and a second snake came with a herb in its mouth and laid it on the first, and the dead snake moved. He took the herb. Athena, some say, gave him two vials of the Gorgon's blood: from the left side, a poison that kills; from the right side, a medicine that brings back the dead.

He used the right side. He raised a young man killed in a chariot accident, then another, then more. The order of the world began to wobble. Hades complained to Zeus that the dead were walking out of his kingdom. And Zeus, who keeps the boundaries, struck Asclepius with a thunderbolt and killed him.

Apollo, in grief, could not touch Zeus, so he killed the Cyclopes who had forged the thunderbolt, and was sent to serve a mortal king for a year as punishment. In the end Zeus set Asclepius among the stars as the Serpent-Bearer, a man holding a great snake in both hands.

His temples spread across Greece. The sick slept in them overnight, among harmless yellow snakes, and waited for a dream that would tell them the cure. His staff, a plain stick with one serpent wound around it, is still painted on ambulances. The story keeps both halves: the gift of healing is real, and so is the line it must not cross.
