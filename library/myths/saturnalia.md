# The Saturnalia

The Romans believed that long ago, before Jupiter, the old god Saturn had ruled in Italy. He had come there as an exile, and the king of Latium had taken him in, and Saturn had taught the people to farm. His reign was the Golden Age. There were no slaves then and no private property. Nobody locked a door, because nobody owned anything worth stealing. The earth gave enough for everyone without being forced.

Then the age ended, as ages do, and the world became the one we know. But once a year, in December, when the days were shortest and the fields asleep, Rome let the Golden Age come back for a little while.

On the seventeenth of the month the priests sacrificed at Saturn's temple below the Capitol. The statue of the god inside had its feet bound with woollen bands all year. On this day they untied them. Then a public feast was held, and the cry went up all over the city: *Io, Saturnalia!*

For the next several days, everything was turned on its head. The togas were put away and everyone wore loose, bright dinner clothes, and the soft felt cap that freed slaves wore. Gambling with dice, banned the rest of the year, was allowed in the street. Schools closed. The courts closed. Wars, in theory, could not be declared. In many houses the slaves sat down to dinner and the masters waited on them, or everyone ate at the same table, and slaves could say what they liked to their owners without being punished.

Each household chose a king of the Saturnalia by throwing dice, and whoever won could order everyone else to do anything: sing, dance, carry the flute-girl around the room, jump into cold water. People gave each other small presents, wax candles and little clay figures, and some of the presents were jokes.

Not everyone loved it. One Roman writer complained that the whole city was in a sweat. Another had a quiet room built at the far end of his villa so that he could work through the holiday without hearing his household celebrate. The poet Catullus called the Saturnalia the best of days.

Then the festival ended, the bands went back around the god's feet, and the masters were masters again. The old order was not really overturned. That was the point. The Romans needed a week a year in which it was openly admitted that the order was not the only way things could be, and that the old king remembered a time before it.
