# Hina Goes to the Moon

Hina was the finest maker of tapa cloth in all the islands. Tapa is made from the inner bark of the paper mulberry tree. You strip the bark, soak it, and then beat it on a wooden anvil with a heavy, grooved mallet, hour after hour, until the fibres spread and felt together into a sheet as soft as skin. Hina's cloth was the whitest and finest, and so everyone wanted it, and so she was always beating.

She beat tapa in the morning while the sun came up over the reef. She beat it through the hot middle of the day while others slept. Her husband wanted it for his cloak and his bed. Her brothers wanted it for gifts. Her children wanted it and needed feeding besides. The sound of her mallet went on and on, *tok, tok, tok*, and in the evening, when she hung the new sheets to dry, there was always water to fetch and fires to tend and more bark soaking for tomorrow.

One evening she carried her calabash down to the stream for water and looked up. The moon was rising, full and white and quiet. Nobody up there was calling her name. Nobody wanted anything.

"I am tired," Hina said, out loud, to no one. "I will go there."

Some tell it that she climbed a rainbow. Some say she set off in her canoe and paddled until the sea ran up into the sky. In one telling, her husband saw her going and ran after her and caught hold of her leg, and it broke off in his hand, and so she went to the moon lame. In every telling, she went, and she took her things with her: her calabash, her anvil board, her mallet.

She lives there still. When the moon is full and bright, the old people say, you can see her sitting in it with her calabash beside her, and the pale patches are her tapa, spread out to dry. On clear nights some people say they can hear, very faint, the *tok, tok* of her mallet. She still beats cloth. But now she beats it for herself, at her own pace, and when she wants to stop, she stops.

The story is told in many forms across Hawai'i, Tahiti, Aotearoa and the other islands, and in some of them Hina is also the goddess of the moon itself. What stays the same is the woman who worked until she could no longer hear herself, and who looked up one evening and chose a place where she could.
