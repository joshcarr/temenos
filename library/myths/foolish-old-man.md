# The Foolish Old Man Who Moved the Mountains

North of two great mountains, Taihang and Wangwu, there lived an old man called Yugong. The name means something like the Foolish Old Man. He was nearly ninety. His house faced the mountains, and every time his family wanted to go south, to market or to visit, they had to walk the long way round, a journey of days.

One evening he gathered his family and said, "Let's move the mountains. We'll level them and make a straight road to the south."

His sons and grandsons agreed. His wife was doubtful. "You're not strong enough to flatten a small hill," she said. "What will you do with Taihang and Wangwu? And where will you put all the earth and stones?"

"We'll carry them to the edge of the Bohai Sea," they said, "past the northern shore."

So the old man set out with three of his sons and grandsons who could carry a load. They broke the rock with hammers, dug the earth with hoes, and carried it in baskets and carrying-poles to the sea. A neighbour's son, a boy of seven or eight who had lost his father, skipped along to help. The journey to the sea and back took them from winter to summer. That was one trip.

A clever old man called Zhisou, the Wise Old Man, lived by the river bend, and he laughed at them. "How can you be so foolish? At your age, with the strength you have left, you can't even pull up the grass on the mountain, let alone move the earth and stone."

Yugong sighed a long sigh. "You're so set in your ways you can't see. You have less sense than that widow's little boy. When I die, my sons will carry on. My sons will have sons, and their sons will have sons, and those sons will have sons. Sons and grandsons without end. But the mountains won't grow any taller. Why shouldn't we level them?"

The Wise Old Man had nothing to say.

The mountain spirit who held the snakes heard all this and was worried that the old man really would never stop. He told the Emperor of Heaven. The Emperor was moved by Yugong's steadiness, and he sent two giant sons of the god Kua'e, who each lifted a mountain onto his back. One was set down in the east, the other in the south. From that day, between the south of Ji and the banks of the Han river, there has not been so much as a small hill in the way.

The story comes from the *Liezi*, a Taoist book more than two thousand years old. People in China still use "the foolish old man moves mountains" for any work that looks impossible and is done anyway, one basket at a time.
