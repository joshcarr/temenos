# The Fisher King

Parsifal grew up in the forest with his mother, who had lost her husband and her other sons to fighting and wanted to keep this last one far from knights. But one day knights rode through the forest in shining armour, and the boy, who had never seen such a thing, thought they were angels. Nothing would keep him after that. He went to King Arthur's court, clumsy and ignorant, and on the way an old knight took him in and taught him how to fight and how to behave. Among the lessons was this: a good knight does not ask too many questions.

Riding alone one evening, Parsifal came to a river where a man sat fishing in a boat. The fisherman told him where to find a bed for the night, and Parsifal rode on and came to a castle. He was welcomed and led into a great hall, and there on a couch lay the lord of the castle. It was the fisherman. He was badly wounded, through the thighs, so that he could neither ride nor walk, and could only be carried, and his only pleasure was to be taken out to fish. He was the Fisher King.

As they sat at supper, a strange procession passed through the hall. A young man carried a lance, and from its tip a drop of blood ran down to his hand. Two more carried golden candlesticks. Then a maiden came carrying a Grail, a dish or cup so bright that the candles dimmed beside it. They crossed the hall and went into another room, and passed again with every course of the meal.

Parsifal wanted very much to ask what it meant. Who was served from the Grail? Why did the lance bleed? What was wrong with the king? But he remembered he'd been told not to ask too many questions. So he said nothing, and ate, and went to bed.

In the morning the castle was empty. His horse stood saddled in the courtyard. He called out, and nobody answered, and as he rode across the drawbridge it rose behind him so fast his horse had to jump.

Later a woman he met in the forest told him what he had done. If he had asked, the king would have been healed, and the land would have been restored. Because he stayed silent, the king still suffered and the country around him lay waste: fields unploughed, women widowed, knights dead.

It took Parsifal years of wandering to find the castle again. When he did, he asked the question. In Wolfram's version it is simply: "Uncle, what ails you?" And with those words the king was healed.
