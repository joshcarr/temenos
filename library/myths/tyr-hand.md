# Tyr's Hand

Loki had three terrible children with a giantess, and one of them was a wolf called Fenrir. The gods brought the wolf pup to Asgard, meaning to keep an eye on him, but none of them dared feed him except Tyr, the god of war and justice, who was the bravest of them. Fenrir grew every day, and every day he grew more frightening, and prophecies said he would one day bring great harm.

The gods decided to bind him. They made a strong iron chain called Leyding and brought it to the wolf, saying it would be a fine test of his strength. Fenrir let them put it on, stretched, and snapped it. They made a second chain, twice as strong, called Dromi. The wolf thought about it, let them put it on, strained, shook himself, and it flew in pieces.

Then Odin sent a messenger to the dwarves under the earth, and the dwarves made a fetter called Gleipnir. It was made from six things: the sound of a cat's footfall, the beard of a woman, the roots of a mountain, the sinews of a bear, the breath of a fish, and the spittle of a bird. That is why none of those things exist any more. Gleipnir was as smooth and soft as a silk ribbon.

The gods took the wolf out to an island in a lake and showed him the ribbon. Fenrir was suspicious. "There's no fame in breaking a thread like that," he said. "And if it's made with cunning, it won't go on my legs." The gods told him he had broken iron; surely he could break silk. And if he couldn't, well, then he'd be too weak to frighten them, and they'd let him go.

"If you bind me so I can't free myself," said Fenrir, "you'll laugh, and I'll wait a long time for your help. I'll let it go on me only if one of you puts his hand in my mouth, as a pledge that there's no trick."

The gods looked at each other. Nobody wanted to lose a hand. Then Tyr stepped forward and put his right hand between the wolf's jaws.

They bound Fenrir with Gleipnir, and the harder he kicked, the tighter it held. All the gods laughed, except Tyr. He lost his hand.

The Norse called the wrist "the wolf's joint" because of it. Tyr, who had been the only one brave enough to feed the wolf, was also the only one who kept the promise, knowing what it would cost. The story is told in Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda*.
