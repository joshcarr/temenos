# Kronos and His Children

Before Zeus, before Olympus, the world was ruled by Kronos, the youngest of the Titans. He had come to power the hard way. His father, the Sky, had pressed his children back into the body of their mother, the Earth, because he could not bear to share the light with them. The Earth, groaning, forged a sickle of grey stone and asked which of her sons would use it. Only Kronos answered. He waited in the dark, and when the Sky came down to lie with the Earth, he struck. The Sky withdrew, wounded, to the height where it still hangs, and Kronos took the throne.

For a while it was a golden age. The fields gave grain without ploughing. No one grew old in the ordinary way. But Kronos had heard a prophecy, from his own mother and father: as you did to your father, so a child of yours will do to you.

So when his wife Rhea bore their first child, Hestia, Kronos held out his hands for the baby, looked at her once, and swallowed her whole. Then Demeter. Then Hera. Then Hades and Poseidon. Five children went down into the dark of their father's body, alive, and he told himself it was prudence. A king has to think ahead.

Rhea grieved, and then she planned. When her sixth child was due she went to the island of Crete and gave birth in a cave on a mountain. She left the baby with the nymphs and the bees and the goat Amaltheia, whose milk would feed him. Then she wrapped a large stone in swaddling clothes, carried it home, and handed it to her husband. He swallowed it without a glance. He had stopped looking at what he was eating a long time ago.

On Crete, young warriors danced outside the cave, clashing their spears against their shields so that the baby's crying would not carry. The child was Zeus. He grew up strong and hidden, and when he was grown he came back, disguised as a cupbearer, and gave his father a drink mixed with an herb that turned the stomach. Kronos heaved. Up came the stone first, then the five children, grown now and furious, blinking in the light.

There was a long war after that, the Titans against the young gods, and the young gods won. Zeus set the stone Kronos had swallowed at Delphi, at the centre of the world, where travellers anointed it with oil for centuries.

The Romans later gave Kronos the name Saturn and remembered the golden age more than the swallowing. The Greeks remembered both: the harvest and the fear, the sickle that cuts the grain and the father so afraid of being replaced that he ate his own future.
