# Eat, My Coat

Nasreddin Hodja was invited to a wedding feast in the next village. He had been working in his field all morning, and he went just as he was, in his old patched coat with the dust still on it.

Nobody greeted him at the door. The host looked straight past him. The servants seated him at the far end of the room, by the draught, and the dishes went round and round the room and somehow never came near him. After a while Nasreddin got up and slipped out.

He went home, washed, and put on his finest coat, a long one trimmed with fur that he kept for great occasions. Then he walked back to the feast.

This time the host hurried to meet him at the door. "Hodja! Welcome, welcome!" He was seated at the head of the room on the best cushions. The pilaf came to him first, and the lamb, and the sweet pastries, and everyone waited to see what he would take.

Nasreddin picked up the hem of his fur sleeve and dipped it into the soup. "Eat, my coat," he said kindly. "Eat."

The guests stared. "Hodja, what are you doing?"

"Well," said Nasreddin, "when I came in my old coat, I got nothing. When I came in this one, I got everything. So it must be the coat that was invited. I am only making sure it gets its dinner."

There are hundreds of stories about the Hodja, from Turkey to Iran to Central Asia and beyond, and in half of them he is the fool and in the other half he is the only one who isn't. One night a neighbour found him on his hands and knees in the street under a lamp. "What have you lost?" "My key." The neighbour knelt and searched with him for a long time. "Where exactly did you drop it?" "In my house." "Then why are we looking out here?" "Because there's more light out here."

Once he was asked to preach in the mosque. He climbed up and said, "Do you know what I'm going to tell you?" The congregation said no. "Then I won't waste my words on people who know nothing," he said, and went home. The next week they all said yes. "Then you don't need me," he said, and went home again. The third week, half said yes and half said no. "Good," said Nasreddin. "Those who know can tell those who don't."

Sometimes the joke is on the Hodja. Sometimes it is on the room. Usually it is on whoever thought they understood what was going on.
