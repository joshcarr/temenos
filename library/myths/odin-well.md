# Odin at Mimir's Well

The world tree Yggdrasil holds up all the worlds, and it has three great roots. One goes to the land of the gods, one to the land of the dead, and one to the land of the frost giants. Under that third root is a spring, and it is guarded by Mimir, the wisest being there is. Every morning Mimir drinks from the spring out of the Gjallarhorn, and that is why he knows so much. Wisdom and understanding are hidden in that water.

Odin, the chief of the gods, already knew a great deal. He had two ravens, Thought and Memory, who flew over the world every day and came back at evening to whisper in his ears what they had seen. But he also knew what was coming: the twilight of the gods, the wolf breaking loose, the fire at the end. He wanted to understand it. He wanted, more than anything, to know.

So he went to the well and asked Mimir for one drink.

Mimir looked at him. A drink from that spring was not given for nothing. The price, he said, was an eye.

Odin didn't argue. He took out one of his own eyes and gave it to Mimir, who dropped it into the spring. Then Mimir filled the horn and Odin drank, and from that moment he saw further than before. One eye looked out at the world; the other lay in the well, looking down into the depths.

That is why Odin is always shown with one eye, a grey old wanderer in a wide-brimmed hat pulled low, leaning on a staff, turning up at farmhouse doors to ask questions. It was not his only sacrifice for knowledge. Another time he hung for nine nights on the world tree, wounded with a spear, without food or drink, "given to myself," until he looked down, and saw the runes, and took them up screaming, and fell. The runes were the secret of writing and of magic.

Odin kept going back to Mimir for advice. Later, when Mimir was killed in a war between the gods, Odin took his head, preserved it with herbs, and spoke charms over it so that it could still talk to him and tell him secret things.

The stories are in the Poetic Edda and Snorri's *Prose Edda*. Odin is not a gentle god. But in these stories he is the one who will pay whatever it costs to understand, and who counts wisdom worth an eye.
