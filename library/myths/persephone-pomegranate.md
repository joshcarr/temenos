# Persephone and the Pomegranate

The girl who had been Kore, the Maiden, was taken down into the earth by Hades, lord of the dead, and became his queen. In the underworld she had a new name, Persephone, and a throne beside his in a hall where the dead came quietly to be judged. There was no sun there, and no flowers, only asphodel, the grey-green plant of the dead, and the rivers, and the shades drifting like smoke.

Above her, the world was dying. Her mother Demeter, goddess of the grain, would not let anything grow until she had her daughter back, and at last Zeus sent Hermes down to fetch her. Hermes found Persephone sitting beside Hades on his throne. Some tellings say she was sorrowful, longing for her mother. Others say she had grown used to being a queen.

Hades did not argue with Zeus's order. "Go back to your mother," he said to her, kindly. "Don't be sad. I'm not an unworthy husband; I'm Zeus's own brother. And while you're here, you rule over everything that lives and moves, and have the greatest honours among the gods." But before she left, he gave her something to eat: a few seeds of a pomegranate, sweet as honey. Some say he slipped them into her mouth; some say she took them herself. She ate them.

Then Hermes drove her up out of the earth in the black chariot, and Demeter, waiting at her temple in Eleusis, saw them coming and ran to her like a maenad down a mountainside. The two held each other. And then Demeter's heart went cold, and she asked: "Child, when you were down there, did you eat anything?"

Because the law of the underworld is that anyone who eats the food of the dead must return to the dead.

Persephone told her the truth. She had eaten the pomegranate seeds: four, or six, or seven, depending on who tells it. And so a bargain was struck. For part of every year, Persephone would live below as queen of the dead, and for the rest she would live above with her mother. While she is below, Demeter grieves, and the fields lie bare, and it is winter. When she comes up, the earth blossoms.

In the Mysteries at Eleusis, which were celebrated for nearly two thousand years, people who were initiated were shown something to do with this story that they were never allowed to tell. The few hints that survive suggest that they came away less afraid of death.
