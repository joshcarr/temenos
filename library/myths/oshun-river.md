# Oshun and the Seventeen

The Yoruba of West Africa tell how, when the world was new, Olodumare, the high god who lives beyond the sky, sent seventeen orishas down to earth to set it in order. Sixteen of them were male. The seventeenth was Oshun, the orisha of sweet rivers, of love, beauty, fertility and the honey of life.

The sixteen held councils to decide how the world should be run. They met in the forest, and they talked a great deal, and they did not invite Oshun. When she came to the meetings they ignored her. When she spoke they talked over her. They decided she was not needed. Her work was ornament, they thought: brass bangles, perfume, sweet water. The real work was theirs.

Oshun did not argue. She withdrew to her river, sat down by the water and did nothing at all.

At first the sixteen hardly noticed. They went on with their plans. But then the rain stopped falling. The rivers shrank to trickles, and then to cracked mud. Nothing they planted would grow. The cattle grew thin. Women did not conceive, and the ones who did lost their babies. Every sacrifice the sixteen offered was refused. Every plan they made went wrong. They argued and blamed one another, and the world they were building began to fall apart around them.

At last they went up to Olodumare to ask what was wrong.

Olodumare looked at them. "I sent seventeen of you," he said. "I count sixteen. Where is the other?"

They admitted they had left Oshun out.

"Then go back," said Olodumare, "and make it right with her."

So the sixteen went down to the river and found her there, sitting by the dry bed with her mirror and her brass fan, and they begged her pardon. They praised her and asked her to come back to the councils, and they promised that from then on nothing would be done without her.

Oshun accepted. The tellings differ on the rest. In some she was already carrying a child, and said that if it were a boy, all would be well. In all of them she returned. The rain came back, the rivers ran, the fields greened, and the women's bellies swelled.

To this day the Osun River runs through Yorubaland, and every August thousands of people gather at the sacred grove at Osogbo, on its banks, to bring her gifts and ask for children, healing and luck. Without her, the story says, nothing else works. Sweetness was never an ornament. The rest of the world needs it in order to grow.
