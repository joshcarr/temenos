# Thoth Brings the Gift of Writing

Socrates was walking outside the walls of Athens with a young friend one summer afternoon, and they sat down under a plane tree by a stream and got to talking about speeches, and about writing them down. And Socrates said he had heard a story from Egypt.

Near the city of Naucratis, he said, there lived one of the old gods of that country, the one whose sacred bird is the ibis. His name was Thoth. He was the inventor of things. He invented number and calculation, geometry and astronomy, and the board games played with pieces and dice. And above all he invented letters.

In those days the king of all Egypt was Thamus, who lived in the great city of the upper country. Thoth came to him with his inventions laid out like goods at a market, and said they ought to be given to all the Egyptians. The king asked him to explain the use of each one, and as Thoth described them, the king praised some and found fault with others. It took a long time. Then they came to writing.

"This, O king," said Thoth, "will make the Egyptians wiser and improve their memories. It is a medicine for memory and for wisdom."

And the king answered him: "Most clever Thoth, one man is able to invent an art, and another to judge how much harm or good it will do to those who use it. You are the father of letters, and out of fondness you have said the opposite of what they will do. This invention will put forgetfulness into the souls of those who learn it. They will stop exercising their memory, because they will trust in marks made by others outside themselves instead of remembering from within. You have found a medicine not for memory but for reminding. And you give your students the look of wisdom, not wisdom itself. They will read a great deal without being taught, and seem to know much while knowing little, and be difficult company, because they will be wise in their own opinion and not in truth."

Socrates added one more thing. A piece of writing, he said, is like a painting. It looks alive, but if you ask it a question it says nothing back, or only says the same thing over and over. It cannot choose its readers, and it cannot defend itself when it's misunderstood.

His young friend laughed and said it was easy for Socrates to make up stories from Egypt or anywhere else. But he took the point. And the story itself survives, of course, because Plato wrote it down.
