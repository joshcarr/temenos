# The Golem of Prague

In the sixteenth century, the story goes, the Jews of Prague lived crowded into the narrow streets of their quarter by the river, and lived in fear. Rumours were spread about them. Mobs gathered at Easter. And the chief rabbi, the great scholar Judah Loew, decided to make a protector.

One night he went down to the banks of the Vltava with two companions. By lamplight they dug the wet clay and laid it out on the ground in the shape of a man, taller than any of them. Then they walked around it, chanting, seven times one way and seven times the other. The clay grew warm. It began to glow. Water ran out of it, hair sprang from its head, nails grew on its fingers. Last of all, the rabbi wrote a word on its forehead: *emet*, which means truth. Or, some say, he put a slip of parchment under its tongue with the secret name of God on it.

The figure opened its eyes and sat up.

They called it Yossele. It could not speak, but it understood orders, and it was enormously strong. It did whatever it was told. It fetched water and chopped wood. At night it walked the streets of the quarter, watching for anyone who meant harm, and the people of the ghetto slept a little easier.

There was one rule. On Friday afternoons, before the Sabbath began, the rabbi always took the name from its mouth, or rubbed out a letter, and the golem lay down as clay until Sunday. Even a creature made of earth had to be given a day of rest.

One Friday the rabbi forgot. He was already at the synagogue, beginning the Sabbath psalm, when someone ran in shouting. The golem had gone wild. It was tearing up trees, throwing furniture through windows, smashing everything it could reach. Nobody could stop it.

The rabbi ran out into the street and faced it, and reached up and rubbed out the first letter on its forehead. *Emet*, truth, became *met*, which means dead. The golem stopped mid-stride and fell, and lay as a heap of clay on the cobbles.

They say the rabbi carried the pieces up to the attic of the Old-New Synagogue and locked the door, and forbade anyone to go up there. Some say it is still there under old prayer books, waiting for someone who knows the words.

The legend was written down in the nineteenth century, though the rabbi was real. It has been told ever since about every powerful new thing people make to protect themselves: a servant of enormous strength that takes every order literally, and a maker who must never forget to switch it off.
