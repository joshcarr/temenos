# Mulan Goes to War

The old ballad begins with the sound of a loom. *Click, click*, the shuttle goes, and then it stops, and all you hear is a girl sighing at her weaving. Her mother asks what she's thinking about. Nothing, she says. Then she tells her.

Last night she saw the army's call-up lists. The Khan is raising troops on the northern border, and there are twelve scrolls of names, and her father's name is on every one. Her father is old. There is no elder brother to go in his place. So Mulan decides she will go.

She does it the practical way. She goes to the eastern market and buys a fine horse, to the western market for a saddle and blanket, to the southern market for a bridle, and to the northern market for a long whip. In the morning she says goodbye to her parents. By evening she is camped by the Yellow River. She can no longer hear her mother and father calling her name. She hears only the river running, loud and cold.

The next evening she reaches the Black Mountains, and instead of her parents' voices she hears the horses of the enemy whinnying on the far slopes.

The ballad gives the war only a few lines. She crosses ten thousand miles. The frost glints on the iron armour, the night watch strikes its gong. Generals die in a hundred battles, and after ten or twelve years the soldiers who are left come home.

The emperor receives them in his hall and hands out ranks and bolts of silk. He asks Mulan what she wants. She doesn't want a post at court. She asks for a fast camel to carry her home.

Her parents hear she's coming and go out to the edge of the town to meet her, holding each other up. Her older sister puts on her best clothes. Her little brother sharpens a knife to kill a pig. Mulan goes into her old room and sits on her old bed. She takes off her armour and puts on a dress from years ago. She fixes her hair in the window and puts a yellow flower on her forehead.

Then she walks out to her comrades, and they are astonished. Twelve years they marched together and not one of them knew.

The ballad ends with two hares. Hold them up by the ears and the buck kicks and the doe squints, but set them running side by side across the field, and who can tell which is which? Courage, in this story, is not a costume. It went to war in plain armour, did its work, and came home to sit on its own bed and fix its hair in the window.
