# Psyche and the Lamp

There was once a king's daughter called Psyche who was so beautiful that people stopped worshipping Venus and came to worship her instead. Nobody wanted to marry her; they only wanted to stare. Venus was furious, and told her son Cupid to make the girl fall in love with the vilest man alive. An oracle told the king to dress his daughter for her funeral and leave her on a mountaintop, where a monster would take her as his bride.

So Psyche was left weeping on the rock. But the West Wind lifted her gently and set her down in a valley, beside a palace of gold and ivory. Invisible servants brought her food and music. At night, in the dark, her husband came to her. He was kind, and she grew to love him, but he made her promise one thing: she must never try to see his face.

Her two sisters came to visit, saw the palace, and burned with envy. "Your husband is a serpent," they told her. "The oracle said so. He is fattening you up to eat you. Take a lamp and a knife to bed. When he sleeps, look at him, and if he is a monster, cut off his head."

That night Psyche waited until he slept. Then she lit the lamp, took the knife, and leaned over him.

It was no monster. It was Cupid himself, the god of love, the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, asleep with his golden curls on the pillow and his wings folded at his shoulders. His bow and arrows lay at the foot of the bed. Psyche picked up an arrow to look at it, pricked her thumb, and fell even more deeply in love. She leaned closer. And a drop of burning oil fell from the lamp onto the god's shoulder.

He woke. He saw the lamp and the knife, and without a word he flew out of the window. Psyche caught hold of his leg and was carried a little way before she fell. From a cypress tree he told her that he had disobeyed his mother for her sake, and that love cannot live where there is no trust. Then he was gone.

Psyche didn't give up. She went looking for him all over the world, and finally gave herself up to Venus, who set her four impossible tasks: sort a mountain of mixed grain in a night, fetch golden wool from killer rams, fill a jar from a waterfall guarded by dragons, and bring back a box of beauty from the queen of the underworld. With help from ants, a reed, an eagle and a tower, she did them all. In the end Cupid came back to her, Jupiter made her immortal, and they had a daughter named Pleasure.

The story comes from Apuleius's *Golden Ass*, written in the second century.
