# Phaethon Drives the Sun

Phaethon grew up being told that his father was the Sun. His mother, Clymene, swore to it. But the other boys laughed at him, and one of them, a son of Zeus, said he was a fool to believe his mother's stories. So Phaethon walked east, all the way to the edge of the world, to the palace of the Sun, to hear it from his father's own mouth.

The palace blazed with gold and bronze and ivory. Helios sat on a throne of emeralds, surrounded by the Days and the Months and the Years and the Hours, and Spring with her flowers, and Summer naked with a garland of wheat, and Autumn stained with trodden grapes, and Winter with her white hair. The light was so bright the boy had to stop at the door.

Helios took off his crown of rays so his son could come close. "You are my son," he said. "And so that you'll never doubt it again, ask me for anything, and I swear by the river Styx that I will give it to you."

Phaethon asked to drive the chariot of the sun across the sky for one day.

His father regretted the oath the moment he heard the wish. "That is the one thing I would refuse you," he said. "You're mortal, and you ask for something not even the gods can do. Zeus himself, who throws the thunderbolts, could not drive this chariot. The road is steep at the start, and at the top it is so high that even I am frightened to look down, and at the end it drops away sharply. The horses breathe fire and are hard to hold even for me. Choose anything else." But an oath on the Styx cannot be broken, and the boy would not change his mind.

So at dawn Helios rubbed his son's face with ointment against the heat, set the crown of rays on his head, and told him: keep to the middle path, not too high, not too low. Spare the whip; hold the reins hard.

The horses felt at once how light the chariot was. They bolted off the road. Phaethon had no idea where the road was, and couldn't have held them if he had. They climbed so high that the earth froze, then plunged so low that the earth caught fire. Mountains burned. Rivers boiled dry. The skin of the Ethiopians was darkened, the story says, and Libya became a desert. The Earth herself lifted her scorched face and cried out to Zeus.

Zeus threw a thunderbolt. Phaethon fell, burning, like a shooting star, into the river Eridanus. His sisters wept for him on the bank until they turned into poplar trees, and their tears hardened into amber.
