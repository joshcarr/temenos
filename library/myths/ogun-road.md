# Ogun Clears the Road

In the early days, the orisha lived in heaven, and the earth below was new and wild. The orisha wanted to come down and live among the people there. But between heaven and earth grew a forest so thick that no one could pass through it: vines like ropes, trees like walls, thorns as long as fingers. One by one the orisha tried to cut a path. Each came back scratched and tired, having gone only a little way.

Ogun watched them. Ogun knew a secret that none of the others knew. He had learned to work iron. He had found the dark rock, heated it in a fire hotter than any before, beaten it on stone, and made a blade that did not bend or blunt.

He went into the forest with his iron machete and he cut. He cut through the vines and the thorn bushes, and through the great roots, without stopping. Where the others had gone a few steps, he went on for days, and behind him lay a road. It ran all the way down from heaven to the earth.

The orisha followed him down the road he had made, and they were so grateful they offered to make him their king. Ogun accepted, and was crowned king of the town of Ire. But he was never easy with crowns and councils, and one day, the story goes, he came home from a long war, tired and thirsty, and found the people keeping a festival of silence. No one greeted him or offered him palm wine, because no one was allowed to speak. Not knowing why, Ogun thought he had been insulted, and in his rage he struck at the people around him. When he learned his mistake he was so ashamed that he planted his sword in the ground and sank into the earth, or went back into the forest, depending on who tells it.

Ogun is still honoured as the orisha of iron, of the road, of the hunt and of war, and of everyone who works with metal. Blacksmiths, hunters, soldiers, and in modern times drivers, mechanics and surgeons, keep his shrines. He is the one who clears the way, and the one whose strength has to be handled carefully. In Brazil and Cuba, where he travelled with the enslaved Yoruba, he is still called on when a path has to be cut through something that won't open by itself.
