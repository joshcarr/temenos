# Māui Snares the Sun

In the old days the sun moved too fast. It leapt up out of the sea in the morning and raced across the sky and plunged into the sea again before anyone could get anything done. The fishermen had barely launched their canoes before it was dark. The farmers couldn't finish their planting. And Māui's mother, Hina, couldn't dry her tapa cloth. She would beat the bark all morning and spread the sheets out in the sun, and before they were dry the day was gone and the dew came down and soaked them again.

Māui was the trickster of the islands, the one who had already fished islands up out of the sea with his magic hook. He watched his mother take in her wet cloth one evening and decided something had to be done about the sun.

He went to see his grandmother, who lived on the slopes of the great mountain on Maui that the people call Haleakalā, the House of the Sun. Every morning the sun came up over the rim of that mountain, she told him, and it stopped at a wiliwili tree near the summit to eat the bananas she cooked for it before it went on across the sky. She gave him advice and she gave him a magic stone axe.

Māui made ropes, sixteen of them, braided as strong as he could make them. Some say he braided the strongest from his sister's hair. Then he climbed to the top of Haleakalā in the dark and hid by the tree and waited.

At dawn the first of the sun's legs came over the rim of the crater. Its legs were its long rays, and it had sixteen of them, and it climbed up on them like a spider. Māui threw a noose and caught the first. Then the second, then the next, until all sixteen were snared and tied fast to the tree.

The sun thrashed and burned and tried to go back down. Māui struck at it with his grandmother's axe, and broke some of its legs, which is why it has fewer now, and is slower.

"Let me go!" the sun cried. "You'll kill me."

"I'll let you go," said Māui, "when you promise to walk slowly across the sky."

They argued for a long time. At last they made a bargain. For half the year the sun would go slowly, so that the days would be long and the crops would grow and the cloth would dry. For the other half it could go faster as it liked.

So Māui let go the ropes. The sun kept its promise. And Hina's tapa dried in the long summer days, white and stiff and finished, and so did everyone else's.
