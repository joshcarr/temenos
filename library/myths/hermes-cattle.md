# Hermes Steals the Cattle

Hermes was born at dawn in a cave on Mount Cyllene, the son of Zeus and the nymph Maia. By noon he was bored with his cradle. He climbed out and wandered to the mouth of the cave, where a tortoise was grazing on the grass.

"What luck," said the baby. "Hello, lovely. Alive, you're a charm against harm. Dead, you'll sing beautifully." He scooped out the tortoise, fixed reeds and a crosspiece to its shell, stretched a cowhide over it, and strung it with seven strings of sheep gut. He struck the strings and sang, making up a song about his own birth. It was the first lyre.

By evening he wanted meat. He ran all the way to the meadows of Pieria, where the gods' cattle grazed, and cut fifty cows out of the herd of his half-brother Apollo. Then he did something very clever. He drove them backwards, so their hoofprints pointed the wrong way, and he wove himself sandals of tamarisk and myrtle twigs so that his own tracks looked like no footprint anyone had seen. An old man working in his vineyard saw him pass. "You saw nothing," said Hermes, "and heard nothing."

At the river Alpheus he rubbed two sticks together and invented fire-making. He sacrificed two of the cows, divided the meat into twelve portions for the twelve gods, and, though he was hungry, ate none of it himself. Then he threw his sandals in the river, crept home before dawn, slipped through the keyhole like mist, and climbed back into his cradle with the lyre tucked under his arm.

Apollo found the tracks and couldn't make sense of them. The old man in the vineyard gave him a clue, a bird gave him another, and he stormed into the cave. There lay the baby, blankets pulled up, pretending to sleep. "Tell me where my cows are," Apollo said, "or I'll throw you into the underworld." "Me?" said Hermes. "I was born yesterday. I care about sleep and my mother's milk and warm baths."

They took the case to Zeus, who laughed so hard at the baby's lies that he couldn't be angry, and ordered him to show Apollo the cattle. On the way, Hermes took out the lyre and began to play. Apollo, god of music, had never heard anything like it. He forgot the cows. He wanted the lyre. So they struck a deal: the lyre for the herd. Apollo became the god of the lyre, and Hermes the god of herdsmen, of roads and boundaries, of luck and thieves and merchants, and of messages carried between the gods and the world below.
