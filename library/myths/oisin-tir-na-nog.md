# Oisín in the Land of Youth

Oisín was the son of Fionn mac Cumhaill and a poet of the Fianna, the band of warriors who hunted the forests of Ireland. One misty morning, by the shores of Loch Léin, the Fianna were out hunting when they saw a rider coming toward them over the water. She was a young woman on a white horse whose hooves didn't sink. Her hair was gold, her cloak was the colour of the sea, and her name was Niamh of the Golden Hair, daughter of the king of Tír na nÓg, the Land of Youth.

She had come for Oisín. She had heard of his poetry and his courage, she said, and she loved him, and she asked him to come with her to her father's country beyond the western sea, where no one grows old, where there is no sickness and no sorrow, where the feasting never ends.

Oisín said goodbye to his father and his friends, climbed up behind her on the white horse, and they rode west over the waves until Ireland was gone.

Tír na nÓg was everything she had promised. There were orchards heavy with fruit and blossom at once, and music, and hunting, and games, and the two of them were happy. Oisín married Niamh and they had children. He stayed three years, as far as he could tell.

But he began to miss home. He wanted to see his father again, and the Fianna, and tell them where he'd been. Niamh was afraid. She gave him the white horse, and she warned him: whatever happens, don't get down from its back. If your foot touches the soil of Ireland, you'll never be able to come back to me.

He rode over the sea and came to Ireland, and it was wrong. The people were small and weak. The great halls of the Fianna were grassy mounds. When he asked for Fionn mac Cumhaill, people told him that was a name from old stories. Three hundred years had passed.

Riding through a glen, he came upon some men struggling to lift a heavy stone. Oisín leaned down from the saddle to help, and lifted it easily with one hand, but the girth of the saddle snapped, and he fell to the ground. The moment he touched the earth, the white horse bolted west and was gone, and the young warrior became an old man, blind and withered, three hundred years old.

In the later stories, Oisín lives just long enough to tell Saint Patrick about the Fianna, and the old heroes, and the land beyond the western sea.
