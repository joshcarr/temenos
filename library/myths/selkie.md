# The Selkie Wife

On the islands off the north of Scotland, and along the west coast of Ireland, people say that some seals are not only seals. On certain nights, often at midsummer, they come ashore on quiet beaches, take off their sealskins, and dance on the sand as men and women.

A young fisherman was walking the shore one moonlit night and heard singing. He crept among the rocks and saw them: seal-folk dancing, with their skins laid in a pile on a rock. One of the women was so beautiful that he couldn't take his eyes off her. Without quite deciding to, he reached out, took her sealskin, and hid it under his coat.

When the dancers saw him they ran for their skins and slipped into the sea, and the seals swam away. All but one. She searched the rocks, weeping, and then she saw him and knew. She begged him to give it back. He wouldn't. Without her skin she couldn't go home, and so she went with him to his cottage.

She became his wife. She was a good wife, the stories say, quiet, and a good mother to the children who came. But she never laughed the way she had on the beach. She would walk down to the shore in the evenings and stand looking out at the water, and sometimes a big seal would come close in to the rocks and she would talk to it in a language no one else understood. The fisherman kept the skin hidden: in the rafters, under the thatch, in a locked chest.

Years went by. Then one day, when the fisherman was out in his boat, one of the children found something while playing: a strange soft skin, grey and speckled, folded in the roof. Or the child heard the father come in late and hide it, and asked the mother why. In every telling, she found it.

She held it for a moment. Then she kissed her children, went down to the shore, pulled on the skin, and slid into the sea. Her husband came home to an empty house and ran to the beach, and saw a seal far out in the waves, looking back once. She never came home.

But the children, some say, would often find fish left on the rocks for them, and their mother's family of seals would never let their boats come to harm. On Orkney and in Ireland there are families still said to be descended from a selkie, and some of them are born with a little webbing between their fingers.
