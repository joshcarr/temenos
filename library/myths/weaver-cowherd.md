# The Weaver and the Cowherd

High in the sky, on the bank of the Silver River that the West calls the Milky Way, lived the Weaver Girl, a granddaughter of the Queen Mother of Heaven. She wove the clouds. All day she sat at her loom making the bright evening clouds and the soft grey ones, and her cloth was so fine that the sky was more beautiful than it had ever been. She did nothing else. She never rested, never combed her hair, never went anywhere.

On earth, at the same time, there was a poor young cowherd, an orphan whose brother had turned him out of the house with nothing but an old ox. The ox, it turned out, could talk. One day it told him that the daughters of heaven would come down to bathe in a certain lake, and that if he took one of their robes from the bank, its owner could not fly home.

He did it. He took the robe of the youngest and most solemn of the girls, the Weaver, and when the others flew away she was left standing in the water. He gave the robe back, and asked her to stay, and she did.

They married. They farmed and wove together, and they were happy, and they had two children, a boy and a girl. Up in the sky the clouds went thin and plain. Nobody was weaving them.

The Queen Mother found out. She came down in a rage, seized her granddaughter and carried her back to heaven. The cowherd came home to find his children crying and his wife gone. The old ox, dying, told him to take its hide, which would let him fly. So he put the children in two baskets on a shoulder pole and rose after her into the sky.

He was almost close enough to touch her when the Queen Mother drew her hairpin across the heavens behind her, and a river of stars opened up between them, wide and deep and impossible to cross.

They stand on either side of it still. She is the star Vega, he is Altair, and the two little stars beside him are their children. But once a year, on the seventh night of the seventh month, every magpie in the world flies up and makes a bridge across the Silver River with their wings, and the Weaver and the Cowherd cross it and meet for one night. If it rains that night, the rain is their tears.

In China the night is called Qixi, and in Japan it is Tanabata. People write wishes on strips of coloured paper and tie them to bamboo, and many of the wishes are for skill with the hands and skill in love. The story keeps both together: the loom she wouldn't leave, and the love that made her leave it.
